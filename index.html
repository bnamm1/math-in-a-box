The idea

Math isn't just the worksheets you see in school.

It's also puzzles and games: a tower you rebuild one disk at a time, a tile that never repeats, a chocolate bar where the first player can always win. Math in a Box brings that kind of math, the kind found at math festivals and conferences, to children who can't get to those rooms.

Each box holds hands-on puzzles cut from birch plywood, a booklet that explains how to play and then the math behind it, and a few paper puzzles solved with a dry-erase marker. Nothing needs charging, and nothing needs a grown-up who already knows the answer. Kids keep the box, so a puzzle put down tonight can be picked up next month.

"This isn't a test, so you can take your time. Play around a bit before checking the explanations." — from the kit booklet

Designed for shelter life
No internet, no screensEvery activity works on paper and wood. A video is a bonus, never a requirement.
No table neededPieces are small enough for a bunk or a lap. Puzzle pages slide into a clear sleeve so the marker wipes clean.
Explains itselfEach card says how to play, then flips to why it works, so a curious kid can go as deep as they like without a tutor.
Theirs to keepKits are gifts, not loans. Families who move keep the box, and the next box builds on the last one.

How it got here

From a math festival to a shelter, in about a year.

Brandon started the project in the fall of 2025 after a summer hunting for puzzle ideas at math festivals. The question underneath it was simple: the best math he'd ever seen was happening in rooms two miles from home that most kids in the city would never walk into. The box is a way to carry that math out of the room.

  1. Jun–Jul 2025Research and planning. The idea takes shape: who the kits are for, what shelter life demands of them, and what a box could hold.
  2. Aug 2025Puzzle hunting. MAA MathFest in Sacramento and the MoMath Festival in New York City, collecting the puzzles and games that become the first box concepts.
  3. Aug 2025Product development begins. Designing the first full kit for real: which puzzles, how they're cut, what the booklet says, how it all packs.
  4. Dec 2025First prototype. The first box is built, packed, and labeled: a proof that the idea fits in a kid's hands.
  5. Dec 2025Davidson Institute Ambassador. Selected for the Class of 2026, one of 40 nationwide. Ambassador training in progress.
  6. Feb 2026More puzzle hunting. Gathering 4 Gardner (G4G16) in San Francisco. More puzzles, and permission to use several of them.
  7. Spring 2026A makerspace says yes. A cold email to Sequoia Fabrica turns into a free Member-Scholar membership and access to a laser cutter.
  8. Aug 2026More puzzle hunting. MAA MathFest 2026 in Boston. New puzzles and games for the kits ahead.
  9. Aug 2026Pilot and sponsor. LifeMoves agrees to a pilot at two family shelter sites; Proof School becomes fiscal sponsor. Kits 1 and 2 are built and bagged.
  10. Aug 2026Aria Krishnamoorthy joins the team as curriculum lead.

  14. 2027Scaling up. Kits reach San Francisco shelters through Children of Shelters, more sites join, and the monthly curriculum begins.
The research

Why a box, and not a tutoring program.

In the summer of 2025, Brandon spent eight weeks on one problem: STEM education for children experiencing homelessness in San Francisco. He defined it, gathered the numbers, looked at what had already been tried, and scored three possible responses against each other before settling on kits. This is what that work found.

The problem, in numbers

  • Over 3,000 students in San Francisco public schools are identified as homeless each year, many living in shelters, cars, or doubled-up housing.
  • Students experiencing homelessness are about three times as likely to be chronically absent.
  • Statewide, they trail their peers by roughly 20 points in math and English proficiency, and their post-pandemic recovery has been slower.
  • Young people without a high school diploma are four to five times more likely to experience homelessness as adults.
Drawn from the 2024 San Francisco Point-in-Time Count, California student data, and Chapin Hall research.

What hasn't worked

  • After-school STEM programs assume stable housing, transportation, and free afternoons. Kids on shelter schedules or long commutes can't attend regularly, so programs open to everyone quietly exclude them.
  • One-off "exposure days" from tech companies rarely coordinate with schools or shelters and rarely follow up. The most vulnerable students often aren't invited or can't get there.
  • Online courses and coding bootcamps need a laptop, internet, and a quiet place. For students without them, remote learning widened the gap instead of closing it.

What a kit has to be

  • Works with no internet, no screen, and no tutor, because none of those can be assumed.
  • Small enough for a bunk or a lap, because a table can't be assumed either.
  • Built around puzzles and hands-on play, the way well-resourced schools teach young children, because problem-solving matters more than any particular math topic.
  • A gift, not a loan, because families move and a kit should move with them.
Existing kit makers Brandon studied: MEL Science, KiwiCo, Steve Spangler Science, Circuit Mess, and KinderLab's robotics kits for shelters.

Get involved

Three ways to help a box reach a kid.

Host kits at your sites

Shelters, family programs, and school partners in the Bay Area: we bring assembled kits, you hand them to families. We ask for two things back, a rough head count and honest feedback: was it fun, was anything confusing, do they want the next one?

Start a conversation →

Fund materials

Plywood, markers, sleeves, and printing run about $15 per kit. A gift of $375 puts Kit 1 into every family at one shelter site; $1,500 covers a hundred kits. Donations run through our fiscal sponsor, Proof School.

Design, cut, or test

Students who want to design a puzzle, makers with laser or 3D-printer time, and families with a seven-to-twelve-year-old willing to try a prototype and tell us what's confusing.
